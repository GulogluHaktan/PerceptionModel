\begin{abstract}
%%%%%%%%% ABSTRACT %%%%%%%%%
Small robots benefit from a single onboard multi-task perception model. However, when one application needs much higher precision on one task, the usual remedies are a separate task-specific network or fine-tuning of the shared model, which degrades the other tasks. A demanding example is sub-pixel gate corners for an EKF state estimator in drone racing. We propose to keep the general model completely frozen and extend it with small plug-in modules that only read its intermediate features, so that removing a module restores the original model bit-for-bit. Our core is an $\sim$8M-parameter FastViT-SA24 hybrid student with segmentation, inverse-depth, lane and gate-corner heads. We add three modules on its frozen stride-8 features (CornerRefiner, CornerPeakHead, NearGateHead) and two parameter-free steps: global duplicate suppression and a minimum-size filter. All modules are selected in the closed loop of the application. We swap only the detector of a third-party racing stack in Isaac Lab \emph{simulation}. Over 128 flights on never-used test seeds, our system succeeds in 87.5\% of flights versus 82.0\% for the dedicated GateNet detector. The difference is consistent across two independent tests but not significant (sign test $p\approx0.14$). The general metrics stay bit-identical, \eg TuSimple IoU 0.803 and SUN RGB-D mIoU 0.749. The reparameterized core runs in 26.4\,ms (15\,W) and 14.6\,ms (MAXN) on a Jetson Orin NX. In our study, offline metrics did not predict closed-loop success, whereas duplicate detections and pixel precision did.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%% INTRODUCTION %%%%%%%%%
Small autonomous robots such as drones and ground vehicles must perceive their surroundings with an onboard computer of a few watts. They are increasingly built around a \emph{single} multi-task perception model. One shared network that predicts semantic segmentation, inverse depth, lanes and object keypoints is attractive on an edge device such as the NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX. It needs one set of weights in memory and one forward pass per frame. It also offers one energy budget and one artifact to validate, update and maintain. Training such a model on many real and synthetic datasets, and distilling it into a compact student, yields a general perception core that serves many applications at once.

This promise breaks when one application needs much higher precision on a single task than a general model provides. Autonomous drone racing is a demanding example. Vision-based racing systems detect gate corners and fuse them in a filter-based state estimator to correct drift and localize relative to the track~\cite{Foehn2020AlphapilotAutonomous}. Learned racing policies push quadrotors to their physical limits~\cite{Song2023ReachingLimit}. At such speeds, the estimator tolerates only one to two pixels of corner error. Practitioners usually respond in one of two ways. The first is to deploy a separate task-specific network, which adds a second model to the onboard budget and gives up the single-model design. The second is to fine-tune the shared model on the target task. Fine-tuning causes catastrophic forgetting, so performance on the other tasks drops when the weights are specialized~\cite{Kirkpatrick2016OvercomingCatastrophic,Wang2023ComprehensiveSurvey}. Multi-task learning has a related problem: re-weighting losses toward one task trades accuracy on the others and does not reliably improve the target task~\cite{Xin2022DoCurrent,Lin2023DualbalancingMultitask}.

We take a third route. We keep the general multi-task model \emph{completely frozen} and extend it with small plug-in modules that only \emph{read} its intermediate features. Each module adds a capability or extra precision for one application and never writes to the core. Removing a module therefore restores the original model bit-for-bit, so the general metrics cannot regress by construction. This property is stronger than what common parameter-efficient methods provide. Prompt tokens and adapters keep the backbone weights fixed but still alter its forward pass~\cite{Jia2022VisualPrompt,Chen2022AdaptformerAdapting,Chen2022VisionTransformer}. Weight-space interpolation produces a new weight set rather than the original model~\cite{Wortsman2021RobustFinetuning}.

A second lesson shapes how we evaluate modules: offline metrics can be misleading. In the instance we study, a module that improved offline crop metrics such as found rate and corner ordering could still collapse the closed loop. One gate detected twice was enough to crash the estimator. We therefore judge every module in the closed loop of the target application. We choose settings on separate validation seeds and report results on never-used test seeds.

We study one instance of this approach in simulation. The core is an $\sim$8M-parameter hybrid FastViT-SA24 student with heads for segmentation, inverse depth, lanes and dense gate corners. When reparameterized, it runs at 26.4\,ms per frame on a Jetson Orin NX in 15\,W mode. The application is a third-party drone racing stack in Isaac Lab, made of a learned racing policy and an EKF state estimator. The stack comes with its own simulator-trained gate detector, GateNet, which serves as our baseline. We swap only the detector; the policy, estimator and camera pipeline stay unchanged.

Fine-tuning the core on simulator renders gave no closed-loop gain within loop noise. So did higher corner loss weights, a stride-2 corner head and model soups, and their cost on general metrics grew with the corner weight. Diagnosis in the loop showed that the gap to GateNet came from pixel precision rather than recall. We closed it with three modules on frozen stride-8 features. A \emph{CornerRefiner} performs second-pass sub-pixel refinement. A \emph{CornerPeakHead} confirms weak detections. A \emph{NearGateHead} handles gates that fill the frame. Two parameter-free filters complete the pipeline: global duplicate suppression and a minimum-size filter.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \emph{frozen-core, plug-in} design for extending a general multi-task perception model. Modules only read intermediate features, so general metrics are preserved exactly. For example, TuSimple lane IoU stays at 0.803 and SUN RGB-D mIoU at 0.749 with and without the modules.
    \item A closed-loop study of drone racing in simulation, with validation and test seeds kept separate. It shows that offline detection metrics did not predict closed-loop success. Pixel-level corner precision and duplicate-free outputs mattered more than found rate.
    \item Evidence that the frozen core plus light modules can match a dedicated, application-trained detector in closed loop. On two independent sets of never-used test seeds (128 flights per arm), our system completes 87.5\% of flights versus 82.0\% for GateNet. The difference is consistent across both tests but not significant at $p<0.05$ (sign test $p\approx0.14$). The core also keeps its general abilities, including 0.987 of real-gate corners within 20\,px at a never-seen real location, where GateNet reaches 0.776.
\end{itemize}
All closed-loop results are in simulation. We study one application against one dedicated baseline and make no claims about real flights or other applications.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}
%%%%%%%%% RELATED WORK %%%%%%%%%
\paragraph{Multi-task and efficient perception backbones.}
A shared encoder with several task heads is a natural fit for resource-constrained robots. It amortizes computation across segmentation, depth, lanes and keypoints. The central difficulty is interference among tasks. Loss and gradient scales differ, and the dominant task can degrade the others. Many multi-task optimization methods address this by re-weighting losses or manipulating gradients. Dual-balancing~\cite{Lin2023DualbalancingMultitask}, for instance, applies a logarithmic transform to each loss and normalizes gradient magnitudes. However, a large-scale study found that specialized multi-task optimizers often fail to improve over a well-tuned weighted sum of losses~\cite{Xin2022DoCurrent}. This suggests that per-task accuracy in a shared model is bounded by a real trade-off and not just by the choice of optimizer. Our experience is consistent with this view. Raising the corner loss weight (10, 40 or 100) increased the cost on general metrics without a measurable closed-loop gain. We therefore leave the multi-task balance of the core as it is. Precision is added outside the core, through modules that read its features, so the general tasks are untouched.

\paragraph{Extending pretrained models without forgetting.}
Fine-tuning a pretrained network on a new objective overwrites weights that earlier tasks rely on, which leads to catastrophic forgetting. Regularization methods such as elastic weight consolidation slow down learning on weights important for old tasks~\cite{Kirkpatrick2016OvercomingCatastrophic}. The continual learning literature frames the broader problem as a trade-off between stability and plasticity~\cite{Wang2023ComprehensiveSurvey}. Weight-space ensembling such as WiSE-FT~\cite{Wortsman2021RobustFinetuning} interpolates between a pretrained and a fine-tuned model to recover robustness, but the result is still a modified set of weights.

Parameter-efficient transfer methods keep the backbone weights frozen and train a small number of new parameters. Visual Prompt Tuning~\cite{Jia2022VisualPrompt} prepends learnable tokens to the input sequence. AdaptFormer~\cite{Chen2022AdaptformerAdapting} inserts lightweight parallel modules into the MLP blocks of a ViT. ViT-Adapter~\cite{Chen2022VisionTransformer} adds a dense-prediction adapter that injects image priors into a plain ViT. These methods reduce storage per task and can match or exceed full fine-tuning. However, prompts and adapters placed inside the network change the activations of the original forward pass, so the original outputs are not guaranteed to be preserved. They are also evaluated mainly on offline benchmarks.

Our modules differ in two ways. First, they only read frozen intermediate features and never feed back into the core, so removing them restores the original model bit-for-bit. Second, we select and validate them in the closed loop of the application. As a point of comparison, in our setting fine-tuning, stronger corner losses, a stride-2 head and model soups all failed to improve closed-loop success.

\paragraph{Gate detection and perception-in-the-loop for drone racing.}
Vision-based autonomous drone racing couples a gate detector with state estimation and planning or control. AlphaPilot~\cite{Foehn2020AlphapilotAutonomous} uses learned gate detection with nonlinear filtering. It exploits multiple simultaneous gate detections to compensate for drift and build a global gate map. Song~\etal~\cite{Song2023ReachingLimit} show that reinforcement learning policies can outperform optimal control in racing because they optimize a task-level objective directly. Their result highlights that closed-loop performance depends on factors that intermediate objectives do not capture.

The racing stack we build on follows this pattern: a learned policy is fed by an EKF that fuses a map prior with gate-corner observations. The stack ships with its own detector, GateNet, trained on simulator data. Such dedicated detectors are accurate in distribution, but they are single-purpose and add a separate network to the onboard budget. In our offline evaluation at a never-seen real location, GateNet places 0.776 of corners within 20\,px, compared with 0.987 for our general core.

Offline detection metrics also miss failure modes that matter in the loop. In our study, duplicate detections of a single gate crashed the estimator even when offline found rate and corner ordering looked favorable. GateNet is the only method we compare against directly. We replace only the detector, keep the policy, estimator and camera pipeline fixed, and report closed-loop success on never-used seeds.

\Cref{fig:framework} gives an overview of the approach. We first formalize frozen-core extension (\cref{sec:formulation}). We then describe the general core (\cref{sec:core}), the plug-in modules for gate corners (\cref{sec:modules}) and the parameter-free post-processing (\cref{sec:postproc}).

\subsection{Problem formulation: frozen-core extension}
\label{sec:formulation}
Let $x$ be an input image and let $f_\theta$ be the general multi-task model with parameters $\theta$. We write $f_\theta$ as a shared encoder--decoder $\phi_\theta$ that produces fused intermediate features, followed by task heads $h_k$:
\begin{equation}
  F = \phi_\theta(x), \qquad y_k = h_k(F), \quad k \in \mathcal{K},
  \label{eq:core}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{K} = \{\text{seg}, \text{inv.\ depth}, \text{lane}, \text{gate corner}\}$ and $F$ denotes the stride-8 fused feature map. All parameters $\theta$, including those of the heads, are frozen once the core is released.

A \emph{plug-in module} $g_\psi$ with its own parameters $\psi$ reads $F$, and optionally the core's own detections $y_{\text{gate}}$, and outputs an application-specific prediction
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\text{app}} = g_\psi\big(\operatorname{sg}(F),\, y_{\text{gate}}\big),
  \label{eq:module}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ is a stop-gradient. During training only $\psi$ is updated, by minimizing an application loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{app}}(\hat{y}_{\text{app}}, y^\star)$ against application targets $y^\star$. No gradient reaches $\theta$. Because $g_\psi$ never writes into $\phi_\theta$ or any $h_k$, the general outputs satisfy
\begin{equation}
  h_k\big(\phi_\theta(x)\big)\big|_{\text{with } g_\psi} \equiv h_k\big(\phi_\theta(x)\big)\big|_{\text{without } g_\psi}, \quad \forall x,\ \forall k \in \mathcal{K}.
  \label{eq:nonregression}
\end{equation}
\Cref{eq:nonregression} is an identity of computation, not an empirical observation. The general outputs are produced by the same operations on the same weights, so every general metric is unchanged by construction. Removing $g_\psi$ restores the original model bit-for-bit. Several modules $g_{\psi_1},\dots,g_{\psi_M}$ can be attached in parallel without interacting through the core.

\paragraph{Closed-loop selection.}
For our application the quantity of interest is the success rate of the full robot system, not an offline score. Let $c$ be a configuration (a set of modules plus post-processing settings), and let $S(c; \mathcal{D})$ be the fraction of successful flights over a seed set $\mathcal{D}$. We choose
\begin{equation}
  c^\star = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{C}} S(c; \mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}),
  \label{eq:selection}
\end{equation}
and report $S(c^\star; \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}})$ on disjoint, never-used seeds $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$. Offline metrics serve only as diagnostics.

\subsection{General multi-task core}
\label{sec:core}
The core is a multi-task student built on a FastViT-SA24 hybrid backbone with $\sim$8M parameters and a 448\,px input. It is trained and distilled on mixed real and synthetic data. The data cover segmentation sets from city, indoor, off-road and aerial domains, TuSimple lanes, and real and simulated racing gates. The core has four heads: semantic segmentation, inverse depth, lanes, and a dense gate-corner head that operates at stride 4 with a single size level of 56 and returns the top-$K{=}8$ gate candidates per frame. For deployment, the hybrid backbone is structurally reparameterized and exported with TensorRT (\cref{sec:edge}). We refer to this model as \emph{release v4}. It is frozen for all experiments in this paper, and every general metric we report (\cref{sec:general}) is measured on this exact set of weights.

\subsection{Plug-in modules for sub-pixel gate corners}
\label{sec:modules}
The closed-loop diagnosis (\cref{sec:ablation}) showed that the frozen core finds gates but localizes their corners too coarsely for the EKF. The median in-loop corner error was 3.5\,px versus 0.97\,px for GateNet, and the error grew with apparent gate size. We address this with modules that all read the frozen stride-8 fused features $F$ (\cref{fig:framework}, center).

\paragraph{Synthesised single-corner peaks (step B).}
As a first, purely output-side step, we synthesise single-corner peaks from the core's dense corner predictions. This makes it possible to emit a corner even when only part of the gate's evidence is strong. The step uses no new trainable parameters on the core.

\paragraph{CornerRefiner.}
The CornerRefiner runs a second pass around each detected gate. Given the initial corner locations from the core's dense head, it reads $F$ in the neighborhood of each corner and predicts a sub-pixel correction of that corner. It thus turns the coarse output of the stride-4 head into the sub-pixel measurements the estimator expects.

\paragraph{CornerPeakHead (cpk3).}
The CornerPeakHead predicts a corner peak heatmap from $F$. Its role is confirmation: a weak core detection is kept only if it is supported by corner peaks. This allows a low detection threshold of 0.05 for the core's gate head, which recovers gates with low confidence without admitting unsupported candidates.

\paragraph{NearGateHead (ng2).}
Corner error is largest for gates that fill the frame. Gates larger than 250\,px had a 26\,px median error for the core at step B, compared with 9.4\,px for GateNet. The NearGateHead is a dedicated detector for such near, frame-filling gates. It operates on $F$ with a confidence threshold of 0.6, and its detections are merged with those of the core.

We combine the core detections, filtered by CornerPeakHead and refined by CornerRefiner, with the NearGateHead detections, and call this configuration \emph{val\_H}. None of the modules changes $\theta$, so \cref{eq:nonregression} holds for any subset of them.

\subsection{Parameter-free post-processing}
\label{sec:postproc}
\paragraph{Global duplicate suppression.}
Detections now come from several sources: the core head, the confirmed weak detections and the NearGateHead. One gate can therefore be reported more than once. For the EKF, two observations of the same gate are a severe failure. In our experiments, adding small near-gate detections without deduplication collapsed success to 15.6\%. We apply non-maximum suppression jointly across all sources. For gate boxes $B_i, B_j$ (the bounding boxes of the four corners) we use
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{IoU}(B_i, B_j) = \frac{|B_i \cap B_j|}{|B_i \cup B_j|},
  \label{eq:iou}
\end{equation}
and, visiting detections in order of decreasing confidence, discard $B_j$ if $\operatorname{IoU}(B_i, B_j) > \tau$ for any kept $B_i$, with $\tau = 0.2$. The low threshold reflects that gates on a racing track rarely overlap strongly in the image, whereas duplicates of one gate do. We call this configuration \emph{val\_DS}.

\paragraph{Minimum-size filter.}
Very small boxes yield unreliable corners and are easily confused with decoys. We keep a detection only if
\begin{equation}
  \min(w_j, h_j) \geq s_{\min}, \qquad s_{\min} = 14\ \text{px},
  \label{eq:minsize}
\end{equation}
where $w_j, h_j$ are the box width and height. We chose $s_{\min}$ on validation seeds (\emph{min14}, \cref{sec:ablation}). Neither post-processing step has learned parameters.

\paragraph{Racing stack.}
We use a third-party drone racing stack in Isaac Lab (\cref{fig:pipeline}). It consists of a fixed learned racing policy and an EKF state estimator that fuses a map prior of the gate layout with 2D gate-corner observations. The camera line is also fixed: an 820$\times$616 sensor with 128$^\circ$ HFOV at 30\,Hz, 50\,ms delay, rolling shutter, tilt error, sensor corruptions, and an adaptive 384-pixel crop with derotation. Only the gate detector is swapped. The baseline is the stack's own detector, GateNet, trained on simulator data.

\paragraph{Seeds and success.}
One seed defines a new random track: gate positions, yaw and size, decoy gates, obstacles, the hall, quadrotor parameters and the start pose. Four drones fly each seed for 3000 simulation steps. A flight is successful if all 22 gates are passed without a crash. We estimate the loop noise at about $\pm$10--15 success points for 32 flights. Small differences on a few seeds are therefore not interpretable. We select settings only on validation seeds 31--38 and 41--56 (\cref{eq:selection}). Results are reported on clean seeds that were never used for any choice: 71--78, 91--98, 101--116 and 121--136 during development, and 141--156 and 161--176 for the two final tests with 64 flights per arm each. We compare per-seed outcomes with a two-sided sign test that excludes ties. A seed counts as a win if our arm completes more of its four flights than GateNet.

\paragraph{General-metric benchmarks.}
To verify \cref{eq:nonregression} empirically and to characterize the core, we use the following sets. For gates: a never-seen real place (``red'', 196 frames / 312 gates, corners within 20 and 10\,px, nested-gate found count, false-gate frame rate of the segmentation head) and a real flight video (TII, corners within 20\,px). For lanes: TuSimple (IoU). For segmentation: SUN RGB-D, BDD100K (sky-aware), an ADE20K subset, IDD-AW (adverse weather), RUGD (off-road, held out) (mIoU), and Aeroscapes (drone-class IoU). In the loop we additionally log in-loop corner pixel error and the EKF next-gate position error, binned by true distance.

\paragraph{Estimator error.}
\Cref{tab:ekf} and \cref{fig:ekf} show where the closed-loop difference originates. Up to 8\,m, the median next-gate errors of both arms are almost identical (0.084--0.130\,m). The difference lies in the tails. At 2--4\,m our p90 error is 0.272/0.258\,m versus 0.351/0.407\,m for GateNet, and at 4--8\,m it is 0.301/0.279\,m versus 0.367/0.344\,m. Large estimator errors at near and mid range are the ones most likely to cause a crash at the next gate, which is consistent with the higher success rate. For far gates (8+\,m) the picture reverses. GateNet has lower medians (0.212/0.204 vs.\ 0.241/0.242\,m) and shorter tails (0.500/0.458 vs.\ 0.606/0.571\,m). Far gates appear small in the image, where the min14 filter removes detections and the core's corners carry less precise information. This weakness is consistent across both tests and is an open limitation.

\paragraph{Step A: the frozen core alone is not enough.}
Using only the core's gate head, our arm succeeded in 37.5\% of flights versus 78.1\% for GateNet (seeds 71--78). This is despite the core's much better offline accuracy on real gates (\cref{sec:general}).

  \label{fig:cornererr}

\paragraph{Diagnosis: precision, not recall.}
After step B (synthesised single-corner peaks, 56.2\%), we measured the corner error inside the loop on frames where both detectors found the gate (\cref{fig:cornererr}). GateNet's median error was 0.97\,px (p90 3.3\,px), ours was 3.5\,px (p90 16.5\,px). The gap was present for small gates (0.79 vs.\ 2.65\,px) and largest in absolute terms for frame-filling gates (9.4 vs.\ 26\,px above 250\,px). The EKF needs roughly 1--2\,px. GateNet's small-gate error lies inside this band, while our step-B errors lie above it in every size bin. Since both detectors detect the gate in these frames, the closed-loop gap is due to pixel precision and not to recall. This diagnosis motivates the CornerRefiner (sub-pixel precision) and the NearGateHead (large gates).

\paragraph{Negative result: changing the core does not help.}
Before adopting modules, we tried to obtain precision inside the core. We retrained or fine-tuned it on simulator renders, raised the corner loss weight to 10, 40 and 100, used a stride-2 dense corner head, and averaged weights into model soups. None of these produced a closed-loop gain beyond loop noise, and the cost on general metrics grew with the corner weight. This matches the trade-off reported in the multi-task literature~\cite{Xin2022DoCurrent}. We therefore kept the core frozen.

\paragraph{Steps C and D: modules.}
The CornerRefiner (C) improved corner accuracy, but the loop did not yet improve measurably. Precision alone was not enough while weak and near gates were missed or poorly localized. Adding the CornerPeakHead, which allows the detection threshold to drop to 0.05, and the NearGateHead (D, val\_H) brought the system to about 10 points of GateNet on clean seeds: 75.0 vs.\ 84.4 (seeds 91--98) and 65.6 vs.\ 75.0 (seeds 101--116).

\paragraph{Negative result: duplicates collapse the loop.}
Three variants that looked reasonable offline were catastrophic in the loop: re-ordering corners to an identity order, geometrically sorting near-gate detections, and adding small near-gate detections without deduplication. Success collapsed to 15.6\%. Offline, duplicates per frame were 0.018 for GateNet, 0.054 for val\_H and 0.317 with the small adds (\cref{fig:ablation}a), while found rate and corner order did not reveal the problem. When one gate is reported twice, the EKF receives inconsistent observations of the same landmark and diverges. This is the clearest instance in our study of offline crop metrics failing to predict closed-loop success.

\paragraph{Steps E and F: post-processing.}
Global duplicate suppression at IoU 0.2 (E, val\_DS) raised validation success on seeds 41--56 from 68.8\% (val\_H) to 82.8\%, a gain of 14.0 points. On clean seeds 121--136 it reached 82.8\% versus GateNet's 87.5\%, which is within loop noise. The minimum-size filter (F) was swept on validation seeds (\cref{fig:ablation}b). min14 reached 90.6\% on seeds 41--56, while min8 (81.2\%) and min20 (78.1\%) were evaluated on the smaller subset 41--48. This comparison is not strictly matched and every point lies within about $\pm$12 points of noise. We read it only as evidence that a moderate threshold is preferable: very small boxes give unreliable corners, while a large threshold discards useful far-gate observations. min14 was fixed before the final clean tests.

\paragraph{Lessons.}
Three observations generalize across the steps. (i) In-loop precision, measured in pixels on the frames the estimator actually consumes, was the limiting factor, not offline recall. (ii) The interface to the downstream consumer matters: one duplicate per gate was more harmful than any corner error we observed. (iii) The improvements came from modules and parameter-free post-processing, never from changing the core, so all of them were free with respect to general metrics.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%%%%%%%% CONCLUSION %%%%%%%%%
We have shown that a general multi-task perception model can be extended for a precision-critical application without being changed. The core stays frozen. Small modules read its stride-8 features, and two parameter-free filters adapt the output to the downstream estimator. In a third-party drone racing stack in Isaac Lab, swapping only the detector, this system completed 87.5\% of 128 flights on never-used seeds versus 82.0\% for the dedicated GateNet detector. The general metrics of the core, from TuSimple lane IoU to segmentation mIoU on six benchmarks and real-gate corner accuracy, are bit-identical with and without the modules. The development path also yielded a methodological lesson. Offline crop metrics did not predict closed-loop success. In-loop pixel precision and duplicate-free outputs did, and retraining the core gave no closed-loop gain.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
All closed-loop results are in simulation; we have no real-flight results. We study one application and compare against one dedicated baseline. The advantage over GateNet is consistent across two independent clean tests but not significant at $p<0.05$ (sign test $p\approx0.14$), so we claim parity with a consistent trend, not superiority. Our estimator errors for far gates (8+\,m) are worse than GateNet's. The latency of the modules on the Jetson Orin NX has not yet been measured. Finally, the min-size sweep compares slightly different seed subsets.

\paragraph{Future work.}
The frozen-core principle suggests a self-expanding system. The vehicle would detect distribution shift onboard and generate targeted synthetic data for the failure mode, from 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstructions, simulation and LLM/VLM-driven scenario generation. It would then train a new module, verify it in the closed loop, and plug it in. Because the core is never touched, such a system could extend to new domains such as aerial, satellite and in-cabin NIR imagery without risking existing capabilities. Immediate next steps are real-flight validation, more clean-test flights to resolve the statistical question, a remedy for far-gate precision, and onboard timing of the modules.

\section{Full closed-loop log and seed protocol}

\paragraph{Seed ranges and flight counts.}
Every seed is flown by four drones, so a range of 8 seeds corresponds to 32 flights and a range of 16 seeds to 64 flights per arm. During development we used clean seeds 71--78 (step A, 32 flights), 91--98 (step D, 32 flights), 101--116 (step D, 64 flights) and 121--136 (step E, 64 flights). Validation seeds were 31--38 and 41--56 (64 flights); the min8/min20 sweep used the subset 41--48 (32 flights). The final tests used seeds 141--156 and 161--176 with 64 flights per arm each, 128 in total. No final-test seed was used for any design decision. The step results are listed in \cref{tab:path} and visualized in \cref{fig:path}.

\paragraph{Loop noise.}
A flight's outcome depends on the random track, the quadrotor parameters and the interaction of perception errors with the policy. Repeated evaluations with 32 flights varied by about $\pm$10--15 success points. For reference, the binomial standard error at $p\approx0.8$ and $n=32$ is about 7 points, and track-level correlation among the four drones of one seed increases the effective variance further. This motivates (i) separating validation and test seeds, (ii) using 64 flights per arm per final test, and (iii) reporting a seed-level sign test instead of relying on raw percentage differences. The per-distance estimator errors for both final tests are given in \cref{tab:ekf}.

\section{Negative results and general-metric details}
\label{sec:app_neg}
\paragraph{Core retraining attempts.}
We tried to raise corner precision inside the core in four ways: (i) retraining or fine-tuning on simulator renders of the racing environment, (ii) increasing the corner loss weight to 10, 40 and 100, (iii) a stride-2 dense corner head instead of stride 4, and (iv) model soups of variants.
